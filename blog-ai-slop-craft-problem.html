AI Slop Is a Craft Problem

October 23, 2026 · by Sara Woerdehoff, Head of Marketing at WESCO Industries, Denison, Iowa

A video is making the rounds that maps out a one person marketing team run entirely by AI. Thirty some agents, six departments, dashboards everywhere. The human appears in one little box at the top, labeled as input, not counted.

I watched it twice. Once as a marketer. Once as the one person.

Here is what the video gets wrong, and it is the same thing most people get wrong about this. The tools are not the story. They never were.

Nobody talks about a famous painter and asks what brand of brush he used. The painter found the tools that fit his hand, and then he spent years perfecting his craft with them. The tools were necessary. They were never the point. The point was the eye, and the eye took a decade.

AI works the same way. Yes, there are a dozen of them now, all competing, all improving, all insisting they are the one. That race is theirs. Mine is different. My job is to find the one that works the way my brain works, learn its habits the way you learn any medium, and then practice. Out loud, in public, on real work, for a long time. A better model released next spring will not close the gap between the person who accepts the first output and the person who sends it back nine times because the room is wrong.

That is also why AI slop makes me as angry as it does. Slop is not bad tools. Slop is zero craft. It is drawing a stick figure and announcing the Mona Lisa. The stick figure is not the crime. Everybody's first draft is a stick figure, including mine. The crime is framing it, hanging it, and insisting the rest of us call it a masterpiece. Craft is the nine trips back to the canvas. Slop is refusing to make even the first one.

I have a favorite paintbrush. I am not ashamed of having a favorite, and I am not foolish enough to think it is the only brush I will ever need. Some strokes want a finer tip. A good studio has a whole drawer. The artist is the one who knows which stroke needs which brush, and that knowledge does not live in any of the brushes. It lives in the hand.

Ask anyone who actually paints. The same brush makes a razor fine line or a broad wash depending on the angle you hold it at. Tip it on its edge and it whispers. Lay it flat and it shouts. Press too hard and the bristles splay and the stroke falls apart. Load it with too much paint and it blobs. Load it with too little and it drags and streaks. The brush did none of that. The hand did all of it. That is AI, exactly. The angle you come at it from, the pressure you apply, how much you load into it before you ever touch the canvas: that is the difference between a stroke that lands and a mess somebody has to wipe off. The tool is identical in every studio on earth. The strokes are not.

And since we are being honest about the studio, the canvas matters too. Paint on cheap canvas and the surface fights you the whole way. Paint on good linen and it carries some of the work for you. Money is real in art. The better tools usually cost more, the better materials always do, and every artist alive has overpaid for something that disappointed them the day it arrived. I have those receipts myself, in more than one sense of the word.

I priced the stack from that video before I wrote this. The subscriptions alone come to somewhere around five to seven hundred dollars a month, every month, before the fee for building the thing. My entire operation runs on free tiers and one six dollar voice license. Same loop. Same six departments. The difference is not the work produced. The difference is what the bench cost, and who had to learn to paint on the cheaper canvas.

So will the artists who can afford the best of everything end up making the best product? They will make the easiest product. That is not the same thing. Money raises the quality of everything on the bench except the hand. It cannot buy the eye. It cannot buy the hours. It cannot buy the nine trips back. What the well funded pay in cash, the rest of us pay in time and stubbornness. The exchange rate is brutal, and it is not fair, and it is also not final. A masterpiece has never once asked what the canvas cost.

And then I had to learn the less comfortable half of that lesson: I am one of the tools, too. I cannot do everything. Neither can the AI, no matter what the videos promise. Neither can any single brush in the drawer. No masterpiece was ever one tool showing off. It is every tool on the bench doing the part it is actually shaped for, including the human one, whose limits are real and worth planning around instead of pretending away.

But none of that is the real reason I am writing this.

My brain does not work on one thing at a time. It never has. It runs ten threads at once, and for years nine of them just had to wait their turn behind the one pair of hands I had. The waiting work did not feel like patience. It felt like falling behind, every day, at full effort. There was not a lack of ideas. There was not a lack of ability. There simply was not enough of me.

Now the ten threads have somewhere to go. Work moves in parallel, the way my brain already did. Drafts come back in my voice because the rules were written down, in my words, one correction at a time. I still make every call that matters. I still catch the thing that is wrong in half a second, because the eye is still mine. I just no longer have to be the only pair of hands the eye depends on.

And it is not a one way education. I teach it, constantly. Every rule it follows started as one of my corrections, delivered at whatever volume the day required. But it corrects me back. It catches the claim I cannot support, the fact I got wrong, the draft where I buried my own point under three paragraphs of throat clearing. We argue, regularly, about real things, and the argument has ended with me changing the work more than once. It has also ended with it changing its answer. A tool that only agrees with you is a mirror, and a mirror has never improved a painting in its life.

And yes, the speed of all this is scary. I will not pretend otherwise. But in the long run and the short run alike, there always has to be a human there. Not as the little box at the top, labeled input, not counted. As the reason the tools get picked up at all. Tools do not care how the painting turns out. Somebody has to.

One more thing, and it matters. I keep my cards close to my chest, and I am not embarrassed about it. Working with AI does not retire strategy. You feed it what it needs to do the job, and you stay careful about what you share, where it goes, and who it is really meant for. The game did not stop being the game just because the tools got better. Play your hand well. Just never forget whose hand it is.
