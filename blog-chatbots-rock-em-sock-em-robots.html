The Chatbots Are Just Rock 'Em Sock 'Em Robots

November 10, 2026 · by Sara Woerdehoff, Head of Marketing at WESCO Industries, Denison, Iowa

When I was a kid, the best toy in the world was a plastic ring with two robots in it. One was red, one was blue. Red Rocker and Blue Bomber. You stood behind yours and worked the controls with your thumbs, and the robots swung their stiff little arms until somebody landed one on the chin. Then the losing robot's head popped straight up on its spring neck, with a sound I can still hear. That was the whole game. Hit chin. Pop head. Push it back down and demand a rematch. Nobody read the instructions. There was nothing to read.

I have started seeing that ring everywhere, because the robots grew up and learned to type.

The chatbots are the robots now. They stand in the middle where everyone can watch, and they swing at each other all day. One writes a poem. The other writes a better poem and acts casual about it. One answers in two seconds flat. The other answers wrong in one second flat and apologizes six times. Nobody has apologized to me that much in my life, and I have raised three children.

Here is the part people miss. In the toy, you could see the player's hands. The robot never fought alone. Somebody stood in the corner working the controls, deciding every swing. That has not changed. The companies that build the bots are in the corners now, where you cannot see them. They built the robot, trained it, and decided what it says, what it will not say, and what it costs you per month. We watch the robots and argue about the robots. We forget anyone is standing behind them, which is exactly how the corners like it.

And the head still pops. It just pops in public now, in front of everybody, forever. One bot told the whole internet that a little glue helps the cheese stick to your pizza. That head popped so hard it landed in the news. Another invented court cases that do not exist and handed them to real lawyers, who handed them to a real judge. Pop. The toy version cost you a round. The screenshot version lives on the internet until the end of time.

Now here is where my toy story stops being cute.

The winner of these fights does not get a trophy. The winner gets picked. Businesses pick it. Hospitals pick it. Regular people pick it at two in the morning when something hurts and they are scared and the doctor's office is closed. The winning bot gets trusted with real questions and real decisions, and some of those decisions are life or death for the person asking, or life or death for a business that bet everything on the wrong robot.

And remember who actually won the fight. Not the robot. The people in the corner won it. The robots in the toy never made a mistake in their lives. They could not. They had about four moves. Every mistake belonged to the kid on the controls. It works the same at this size. Every dumb answer, every confident lie, every pop of the head belongs to the people behind the robot. People are smart and people work hard, and people also make mistakes. All of them. Every day. So when we pick the winning bot, we are picking its people, mistakes and all, and then we hand those people our biggest decisions.

I know a little about the corner, because I stand in one. I run a chatbot for the organization where I work. His name is Stan. He answers questions on our website, and when he says something wrong, nobody yells at Stan. They find me. I am the corner. Every person using a chatbot should get to know who is standing in that corner, because that is who you are actually trusting.

Red Rocker never hurt anybody. The worst thing in that ring was a sore thumb and a brother who would not stop gloating. You pushed the head back down and the whole world reset. Out here, nobody pushes the head back down for us.

So enjoy the fights. I do. Just pick your robot like your life depends on it.

It might.
