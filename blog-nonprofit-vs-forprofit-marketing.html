Nonprofit Marketing Is Not For Profit Marketing With Less Money

October 21, 2026 · by Sara Woerdehoff, Head of Marketing at WESCO Industries, Denison, Iowa

People outside the nonprofit world tend to assume the difference is the budget. Smaller organization, smaller numbers, same job. I have been asked, in so many words, whether nonprofit marketing is just regular marketing with the money taken out. By that logic a bicycle is a car with the engine taken out. Technically adjacent. Operationally, you are going to feel the difference in your legs.

I have spent eight years as a one person marketing department in the nonprofit sector, carrying director level scope, and the budget is honestly the least interesting difference. The job is built differently, from the goal up.

Start with what success means. In for profit marketing the line from work to result is short and honest: pipeline, revenue, growth. A campaign either moved the number or it did not, and the number is money. In nonprofit marketing the result runs in two directions at once. Programs have to fill, because empty services help nobody, and money has to arrive, because filled programs with no funding close anyway. A residential census campaign and a year end giving campaign sit on the same desk, aimed at different people, measured in different units, and both of them are the mission. When our homes fill, that is not a sales figure. That is somebody in a safe place tonight.

Then the audiences multiply. A for profit marketer talks to customers, and maybe investors a few times a year. A nonprofit marketer talks to the people served, their families, donors, grant funders, referral partners, regulators, volunteers, and a board. Often in the same week. Occasionally before lunch. Every one of them needs a different truth told a different way. A funder wants outcomes and stewardship. A family wants to know their person will be safe and known by name. A donor wants to feel the gift mattered. Mix those messages up in public and you will spend trust that took years to build, and no, you cannot A/B test your way out of that one.

Mostly, the job is translation. Where I come from, dinner is the meal at noon and supper is the meal at night, and lunch, to plenty of the people I serve, is dinner. Neither word is wrong. The table is the same. But use the wrong one and your audience knows instantly that you are not from here. Sector language works exactly the same way. For profit says customers and pipeline. We say members and census. Same human beings, same full table, different words, and the marketer is the one standing in the middle translating so nobody has to feel like a stranger at their own meal.

The money behaves differently too. Some of ours arrives restricted, given for one purpose and legally fenced off from every other purpose, which is a special kind of fun when the fence is around the wrong field. And some of our biggest resources are not money at all. The largest advertising line I manage is a Google Ad Grant, $10,000 a month in search advertising that comes with a rulebook: keep the click through rate healthy, track conversions properly, follow the policy pages, or the free money gets paused. Free advertising with homework. It is real media with real value, and it cannot be spent the way a normal budget is spent. Nonprofit marketing runs on instruments like that, grants, donated placements, in kind partnerships, and local rates you negotiate yourself, each with its own paperwork and its own talent for disappearing the moment you stop paying attention.

The staffing difference is the one nobody believes until they see it. A for profit company our size, about $12 million in revenue, typically runs a marketing team of four to eight people, usually with an agency around them. Our marketing department is me, at about six tenths of one percent of revenue, no agency. Department meetings are brief and unanimous. I also plan the Christmas parties, for the whole company, because the department party would just be me and a grocery store cake, and I refuse to put that in a report.

That is not a complaint. It is a design constraint, and it changes what good marketing even looks like. When you cannot specialize, you generalize on purpose: strategy, brand, design, web development, video, advertising, analytics, and grant writing in one person, held together by systems doing the work a team would do. The skill is not depth in one channel. The skill is judgment, knowing which channel earns its hours this week and letting the rest wait their turn.

Here is what surprises people, though. The envy runs both ways. For profit marketing has things my world studies wistfully: real testing budgets, clean attribution, salaries that match the market, and the freedom to kill a campaign that is not working without a committee conversation about its feelings. And nonprofit marketing has things the for profit world is quietly trying to learn: how to make people care without a discount code, how to build loyalty that is not a points program, how to tell a true story well enough that someone hands you money for a result they will never personally use. Purpose is not a nonprofit invention that companies borrowed for their slide decks. It is a discipline, and organizations that live on it get very good at it, mostly because the alternative is closing.

So no, the difference is not the budget. The difference is what the marketing is for. One sector sells a product and measures money. The other fills programs, earns trust, and raises the money that keeps the doors open, all at the same time, usually with a department that fits in one office chair. Having done the second for eight years, I would put its training up against any in the business. I can stretch a dollar until it begs, run a campaign in six directions at once, and I remain irrationally afraid of squirrels, which keeps me humble and mostly indoors.

I am Sara Woerdehoff, Head of Marketing at WESCO Industries in Denison, Iowa, a nonprofit marketing leader in western Iowa.
